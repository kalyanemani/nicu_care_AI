# NICU Discharge Frequently Asked Questions for Parents

## What Is Corrected Age and Why Does It Matter

Corrected age (also called adjusted age) is the age your baby would be if they had been born on their due date. It accounts for the weeks of development that your baby missed by being born early.

### How to Calculate Corrected Age
- Take your baby's actual age in weeks since birth (chronological age).
- Subtract the number of weeks your baby was born early (40 weeks minus gestational age at birth).
- The result is the corrected age.

### Example
- Baby was born at 29 weeks gestation on September 28, 2026.
- Due date would have been December 21, 2026 (40 weeks).
- On November 24, 2026, the baby is 8 weeks old (chronological age).
- Weeks born early: 40 minus 29 = 11 weeks early.
- Corrected age: 8 weeks minus 11 weeks = negative 3 weeks, meaning the baby has not yet reached their original due date.

### Why It Matters
- Developmental milestones (smiling, holding the head up, rolling over, sitting) should be evaluated using the CORRECTED age, not the chronological age.
- Growth (weight, length, head circumference) should be plotted on growth charts using the corrected age.
- Using chronological age would make most preterm babies appear significantly delayed when they are actually developing normally for their adjusted maturity.
- Corrected age is typically used for developmental assessment until the child is 2 to 3 years old, after which the difference becomes negligible for most children.

## Common Medications After NICU Discharge

### Caffeine Citrate
- Purpose: Prevents apnea of prematurity (episodes where the baby stops breathing for more than 20 seconds).
- Typical duration: Discontinued before or shortly after discharge if the baby has been apnea-free for 5 to 7 days. Some babies go home on caffeine and stop it under pediatrician supervision.
- If still prescribed at discharge, give the exact dose at the same time each day. Do not stop abruptly without medical guidance.

### Multivitamin with Iron (Poly-Vi-Sol with Iron)
- Purpose: Prevents iron-deficiency anemia, which is very common in preterm infants because they did not receive the full iron stores that transfer from mother to baby during the third trimester.
- Dose: Typically 1.0 mL once daily.
- Administration: Give between feeds for best iron absorption. Can be mixed with a small amount of expressed breast milk if the baby dislikes the taste.
- Side effects: Iron drops cause dark green or black stools. This is normal and expected. Iron drops may also temporarily stain teeth; clean the gums and any emerging teeth with a soft damp cloth after administration.

### Vitamin D Drops
- Purpose: Supports bone growth and prevents rickets.
- Dose: 400 IU once daily for all breastfed infants and formula-fed infants consuming less than 1000 mL of formula per day.
- Administration: Can be dropped directly onto the nipple before breastfeeding or given via a dropper into the cheek.

## Immunization Schedule for Preterm Infants

Preterm infants should receive routine childhood immunizations according to their CHRONOLOGICAL age (actual birth date), NOT their corrected age. This is one of the few areas where chronological age, not corrected age, is used.

### Key Points
- The first hepatitis B vaccine is given before hospital discharge (or at birth).
- Subsequent vaccines (DTaP, IPV, Hib, PCV13, rotavirus) begin at 2 months chronological age, regardless of how premature the baby was.
- Preterm infants may have a slightly higher rate of minor side effects (low-grade fever, fussiness) after vaccination. This is normal.
- Do NOT delay vaccinations. Preterm infants are MORE vulnerable to vaccine-preventable diseases, not less.

## RSV (Respiratory Syncytial Virus) and Palivizumab

RSV is a common respiratory virus that causes mild cold-like symptoms in older children and adults but can cause severe bronchiolitis and pneumonia in preterm infants, potentially requiring rehospitalization.

### Who Receives Palivizumab (Synagis)
- Infants born before 29 weeks of gestation who are younger than 12 months chronological age at the start of RSV season.
- Infants with chronic lung disease of prematurity (bronchopulmonary dysplasia, BPD) who required medical treatment within 6 months of the start of RSV season.
- Infants with hemodynamically significant congenital heart disease.

### RSV Season
- In most regions, RSV season runs from October/November through March/April.
- Palivizumab is given as a monthly intramuscular injection during RSV season (up to 5 doses).

### Prevention Without Palivizumab
- Wash hands thoroughly and frequently before touching the baby.
- Avoid exposing the baby to crowds, especially during RSV season.
- Keep the baby away from anyone with cold symptoms (cough, runny nose, sneezing).
- Do not allow smoking near the baby. Secondhand smoke increases RSV severity.

## Follow-Up Appointments After Discharge

### Pediatrician Visit
- Schedule the first pediatrician visit within 2 to 3 days after hospital discharge. This visit checks the baby's weight (to ensure they have not lost excessive weight since discharge), feeding adequacy, and jaundice levels.

### Neonatal Follow-Up Clinic
- Many NICU programs have dedicated follow-up clinics that monitor developmental milestones, vision, hearing, and growth at regular intervals (typically at 4, 8, 12, 18, and 24 months corrected age).
- These visits are critical for early identification of developmental delays so that early intervention services (physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy) can begin promptly.

### Ophthalmology (Eye) Follow-Up
- Infants born before 30 weeks or weighing less than 1500 grams at birth are screened for Retinopathy of Prematurity (ROP) in the NICU.
- If ROP was identified, the ophthalmologist will schedule follow-up exams after discharge until the retinal blood vessels are fully mature or the ROP has resolved.

### Hearing Screening
- All newborns receive a hearing screen before NICU discharge. If the baby did not pass or the results were inconclusive, a follow-up audiology appointment is scheduled.
- Preterm infants are at higher risk for sensorineural hearing loss. Early detection and intervention (hearing aids, speech therapy) produce significantly better language outcomes.

## Caring for Your Baby at Home: Practical Tips

### Handwashing
- Wash your hands with soap and water for at least 20 seconds before every feeding, medication administration, and diaper change.
- Ask all visitors to wash their hands before holding the baby.
- Use alcohol-based hand sanitizer (at least 60 percent alcohol) when soap and water are not available.

### Limiting Visitors
- For the first 2 to 3 months after discharge (and especially during RSV season), limit visitors and avoid crowded indoor spaces.
- Anyone who is sick — even with a mild cold — should not visit or hold the baby.
- Siblings who attend daycare or school should wash their hands and change clothes immediately upon arriving home before interacting with the baby.

### Temperature Monitoring
- Take the baby's temperature rectally (most accurate for infants) if they feel warm, cool, or are unusually fussy.
- Normal rectal temperature range: 36.5 to 37.5 degrees Celsius (97.7 to 99.5 degrees Fahrenheit).
- Contact the pediatrician immediately if the rectal temperature is above 38.0 degrees Celsius (100.4 degrees Fahrenheit) or below 36.5 degrees Celsius (97.7 degrees Fahrenheit) in an infant under 3 months of age.

### Bathing
- Sponge baths are recommended until the umbilical cord stump falls off and heals completely.
- After that, tub baths in 2 to 3 inches of warm water (test with the inside of your wrist — it should feel comfortably warm, not hot) are safe.
- Bathe the baby 2 to 3 times per week. Daily bathing can dry out the skin. Use a gentle, fragrance-free baby cleanser.
