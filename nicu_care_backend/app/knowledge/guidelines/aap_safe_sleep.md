# Safe Sleep Recommendations for Infants (Including Preterm)

## Why Safe Sleep Is Critical for Preterm Infants

Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS) and other sleep-related deaths remain a leading cause of postneonatal infant mortality. Preterm and low birth weight infants are at significantly HIGHER risk of SIDS compared to full-term infants — approximately 2 to 3 times higher. This elevated risk makes strict adherence to safe sleep guidelines absolutely essential for every NICU-discharged baby.

## The ABCs of Safe Sleep

The core safe sleep principle is summarized as the ABCs:
- A = Alone: The baby sleeps alone in their own sleep space.
- B = Back: The baby is placed on their BACK (supine) for every sleep.
- C = Crib: The baby sleeps in an approved crib, bassinet, or play yard.

## Sleep Position: Always on the Back

Place the baby on their back (supine position) for EVERY sleep — including naps, nighttime sleep, and any brief rest period. This applies to ALL infants from birth through at least 1 year of age (12 months).

### Critical Rules
- Never place the baby on their stomach (prone) or side to sleep. Side-sleeping is unstable and the baby can easily roll onto their stomach.
- Once the baby can consistently roll from back to front AND from front to back on their own (typically around 4 to 6 months corrected age), they may be allowed to remain in the sleep position they roll into. However, always START every sleep on the back.
- Do not use positioners, wedges, or rolled towels to keep the baby in a specific position. These are suffocation hazards and have NOT been proven to reduce SIDS.

### Addressing Concerns About Flat Head (Positional Plagiocephaly)
- Some parents worry about flat spots on the back of the baby's head from supine sleeping. This is common but benign and usually resolves on its own.
- During supervised AWAKE time, place the baby on their tummy (tummy time) for supervised play. This strengthens neck and shoulder muscles and helps prevent flat spots.
- Alternate the direction the baby's head faces during sleep (left one night, right the next).

## Sleep Surface Requirements

The baby must sleep on a firm, flat surface that meets the safety standards of the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC).

### Approved Sleep Surfaces
- Full-size crib with a firm, tight-fitting mattress.
- Portable crib or play yard (such as a Pack 'n Play) with the manufacturer's mattress only.
- Bassinet with a firm, flat mattress.

### Prohibited Sleep Surfaces
- Adult beds, waterbeds, or memory foam mattresses. These are suffocation risks.
- Couches, sofas, armchairs, or recliners. Infants can become wedged between cushions.
- Car seats, strollers, swings, bouncers, or rockers should NOT be used for routine sleep. If the baby falls asleep in a car seat during travel, transfer them to a firm, flat surface as soon as safely possible.
- Inclined sleepers (such as the Fisher-Price Rock 'n Play) have been recalled due to multiple infant deaths and must NEVER be used.

## Nothing Else in the Crib

The crib should contain ONLY the baby and a fitted sheet. Remove ALL of the following:
- Blankets, quilts, and comforters.
- Pillows and pillow-like toys.
- Stuffed animals and plush toys.
- Bumper pads (including mesh bumpers — they are unnecessary and can pose entanglement risks).
- Loose sheets or unfitted bedding.

## Room Temperature, Keeping Baby Warm Safely, and Thermoregulation

### Safe Room Temperature Range
- The ideal ambient room temperature for a newborn or preterm infant is **20 to 22 degrees Celsius (68 to 72 degrees Fahrenheit)**.
- The minimum safe room temperature is **18 degrees Celsius (65 degrees Fahrenheit)**. If the room drops below this, dress the baby in an extra layer (such as a long-sleeve onesie plus a certified wearable sleep sack) rather than using loose blankets or space heaters.
- For Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC) or undressed skin-to-skin sessions, the room should be slightly warmer: **22 to 24 degrees Celsius (72 to 75 degrees Fahrenheit)** to prevent hypothermia during transfers.
- **Why Preterm Babies Are Sensitive:** Preterm infants have larger surface-area-to-body-mass ratios, thinner skin, and reduced subcutaneous brown fat, making them lose body heat up to four times faster than adults.
- **Preventing Overheating (SIDS Risk):** Overheating is a major risk factor for Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS). Never overbundle the baby. Signs of overheating include flushed cheeks, sweating on the neck or head, rapid breathing, and heat rash. A general rule of thumb is to dress the baby in just one more layer of light clothing than an adult would comfortably wear in the same room.

### Keeping the Baby Warm Without Blankets
- Dress the baby in a wearable blanket (sleep sack) appropriate for the room temperature.
- Room temperature should be comfortable for a lightly clothed adult, typically 20 to 22 degrees Celsius (68 to 72 degrees Fahrenheit).
- Signs of overheating include sweating, damp hair, flushed cheeks, heat rash, and rapid breathing. If you observe these, remove a layer of clothing.

## Room Sharing Without Bed Sharing

The AAP recommends that infants sleep in the parents' room, close to the parents' bed, but on a separate surface designed for infants, for at least the first 6 months and ideally for the first 12 months.

### Why Room Sharing Is Recommended
- Room sharing reduces the risk of SIDS by approximately 50 percent.
- It facilitates breastfeeding, monitoring, and comforting.

### Why Bed Sharing Is Dangerous
- Bed sharing (sleeping in the same bed as the parent) increases the risk of suffocation, strangulation, and entrapment.
- The risk is especially high if either parent smokes, has consumed alcohol, has taken sedating medications, or is excessively fatigued.
- Bed sharing on a couch or armchair is the MOST dangerous sleeping arrangement and must be avoided completely.

## Swaddling

Swaddling may help calm a young infant, but it must be done correctly and discontinued at the right time.

### Safe Swaddling Rules
- The swaddle must be snug around the chest but allow the hips to move freely (hip-healthy swaddling). Tight wrapping of the hips can lead to hip dysplasia.
- Always place a swaddled baby on their BACK.
- STOP swaddling as soon as the baby shows ANY signs of attempting to roll, which may occur as early as 2 months corrected age. A swaddled baby who rolls onto their stomach cannot use their arms to push up or reposition, creating a suffocation risk.

## Pacifier Use During Sleep

The AAP recommends offering a pacifier at nap time and bedtime once breastfeeding is well established (typically by 3 to 4 weeks of age).

### Guidelines
- If the baby refuses the pacifier, do not force it.
- If the pacifier falls out after the baby falls asleep, there is no need to replace it.
- Do not hang the pacifier around the baby's neck or attach it to clothing with a string or clip during sleep — these are strangulation hazards.
- Pacifier use during sleep has been associated with a reduced risk of SIDS.

## Smoking, Alcohol, and Drug Exposure

- Do not smoke during pregnancy or after the baby is born. Secondhand and thirdhand smoke exposure significantly increases SIDS risk.
- Do not smoke in the home or car, even when the baby is not present. Smoke residue on surfaces (thirdhand smoke) is harmful.
- Do not consume alcohol or use sedating drugs if you plan to bed share or fall asleep while holding the baby. Impaired parents have a substantially higher risk of overlaying (rolling onto) the infant.

## When to Worry: Sleep-Related Warning Signs

Contact the pediatrician or seek emergency care if:
- The baby has episodes where they stop breathing for more than 20 seconds (apnea).
- The baby turns blue or pale around the lips, face, or fingernails during sleep (cyanosis).
- The baby is unusually difficult to arouse from sleep and does not respond to gentle stimulation.
- The baby has noisy, labored breathing with chest retractions (pulling inward at the ribs or below the ribcage) during sleep.
