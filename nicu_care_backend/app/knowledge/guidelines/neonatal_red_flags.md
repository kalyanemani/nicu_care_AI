# Neonatal Danger Signs and Red-Flag Symptoms

## Purpose of This Guide

This document lists the clinical danger signs that require IMMEDIATE medical attention in neonates and young infants (birth through 3 months corrected age). These signs indicate potentially life-threatening conditions including neonatal sepsis, meningitis, respiratory failure, necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), and cardiac emergencies. When any of these signs are observed, the caregiver must contact their pediatrician or proceed to the nearest emergency department IMMEDIATELY without waiting.

## Category 1: Breathing Problems (Respiratory Distress)

### Signs to Watch For
- Breathing rate consistently above 60 breaths per minute at rest (tachypnea). To count: watch the baby's chest for a full 60 seconds while the baby is calm and not crying.
- Chest retractions: the skin pulls inward between the ribs, below the ribcage (subcostal), or at the notch above the breastbone (suprasternal) with each breath. This indicates the baby is working very hard to breathe.
- Nasal flaring: the nostrils widen visibly with each breath, indicating air hunger.
- Grunting: a short, low-pitched sound heard at the end of each breath. This is the baby's body trying to keep the lungs inflated and is a sign of significant respiratory distress.
- Apnea: the baby stops breathing for more than 20 seconds, or for a shorter period if accompanied by cyanosis (blue discoloration) or bradycardia (slow heart rate). Brief pauses in breathing (less than 10 seconds) are normal in preterm infants, but prolonged pauses are not.

### What to Do
- Call emergency services or your hospital's NICU emergency line immediately.
- Keep the baby's airway clear: position the baby on their back with the head in a neutral position (not flexed forward or extended backward).
- If the baby is on supplemental oxygen at home, ensure the equipment is functioning and the flow rate is set as prescribed.

## Category 2: Color Changes (Cyanosis and Pallor)

### Signs to Watch For
- Central cyanosis: blue or purple discoloration of the lips, tongue, or inside of the mouth. This indicates low blood oxygen levels and is always an emergency in a neonate.
- Peripheral cyanosis (blue hands and feet only) is common in newborns during the first 24 to 48 hours and when the baby is cold. It is usually benign but should be mentioned to the pediatrician if persistent.
- Mottled skin: a lace-like pattern of blue or purple patches on the skin, especially the trunk and limbs. If new or worsening, this can indicate poor circulation or shock.
- Extreme pallor: the baby appears very pale or gray, which may indicate anemia, poor perfusion, or circulatory shock.

### What to Do
- Central cyanosis (blue lips or tongue) requires immediate emergency medical attention.
- If the baby appears dusky or pale, stimulate them gently by rubbing their back or flicking the soles of their feet. If color does not improve within seconds, call emergency services.

## Category 3: Temperature Instability (Fever and Hypothermia)

### Fever (Hyperthermia)
- A rectal temperature of 38.0 degrees Celsius (100.4 degrees Fahrenheit) or higher in an infant under 3 months of age is a MEDICAL EMERGENCY until proven otherwise.
- Fever in a young infant can indicate serious bacterial infection (sepsis, meningitis, urinary tract infection). These conditions can progress rapidly and are life-threatening without prompt antibiotic treatment.
- Do NOT administer acetaminophen (Tylenol) or ibuprofen to bring down the fever before calling the doctor. The doctor needs to evaluate the baby with the fever present. Ibuprofen is NEVER given to infants under 6 months of age.
- Do NOT use cold water baths, alcohol rubs, or ice packs. These can cause dangerous drops in body temperature and are harmful.

### Hypothermia
- A rectal temperature below 36.5 degrees Celsius (97.7 degrees Fahrenheit) is concerning and may indicate infection, environmental cold stress, or sepsis.
- Rewarm the baby using skin-to-skin contact (Kangaroo Mother Care) and warm blankets. If the temperature does not rise above 36.5 degrees Celsius within 30 minutes, contact the pediatrician.

## Category 4: Feeding Refusal and Gastrointestinal Emergencies

### Signs to Watch For
- Complete feeding refusal: the baby will not suck, swallow, or accept any form of nutrition for 2 or more consecutive feeds.
- Bilious (green) vomiting: vomit that is dark green or bile-colored indicates a possible intestinal obstruction (such as malrotation with volvulus) and is a SURGICAL EMERGENCY requiring immediate evaluation.
- Bloody vomit or bloody stools: may indicate necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) or other serious gastrointestinal conditions, especially in preterm infants.
- Abdominal distension: the belly appears visibly swollen, tight, or shiny. When gently tapped, it may sound hollow. The baby may cry when the abdomen is touched.
- Absent bowel sounds or complete lack of stool output for more than 48 hours in a feeding infant.

### What to Do
- Green vomiting, bloody stools, or severe abdominal distension require IMMEDIATE emergency department evaluation. Do not wait for a scheduled pediatrician appointment.
- Stop feeding and transport the baby to the nearest emergency department.

## Category 5: Neurological Warning Signs

### Signs to Watch For
- Seizures: rhythmic jerking of the arms, legs, or face. In neonates, seizures can also appear as repetitive eye movements, lip-smacking, tongue thrusting, or bicycling movements of the legs.
- Extreme lethargy: the baby is unusually difficult to wake, does not respond to voice or gentle stimulation, and is limp or floppy when picked up (poor muscle tone, also called hypotonia).
- High-pitched, inconsolable cry: a shrill, unusually high-pitched cry that is distinctly different from the baby's normal cry can indicate pain, meningitis, or increased intracranial pressure.
- Bulging fontanelle: the soft spot on top of the baby's head feels tense and bulging when the baby is upright and calm (not crying). This can indicate increased pressure inside the skull.

### What to Do
- Seizures, unresponsiveness, or a bulging fontanelle require immediate emergency transport.
- Lay the baby on their side to prevent aspiration if they are actively vomiting during a seizure.
- Do NOT put anything in the baby's mouth during a seizure.

## Category 6: Dehydration

### Signs to Watch For
- Fewer than 4 wet diapers in a 24-hour period (normal is 6 to 8).
- Sunken fontanelle (the soft spot on top of the head appears depressed or concave).
- Dry mouth and lips with no visible saliva.
- No tears when crying.
- Dark concentrated urine or orange/rust-colored crystals in the diaper (urate crystals are normal in the first 2 to 3 days of life but concerning thereafter).
- Skin that remains tented when gently pinched (poor skin turgor), indicating moderate to severe dehydration.

### What to Do
- Mild dehydration (slightly reduced wet diapers but baby is feeding): increase feeding frequency and contact the pediatrician within the same day.
- Moderate to severe dehydration (sunken fontanelle, no tears, skin tenting, lethargy): proceed to the emergency department. The baby may require intravenous fluids.

## Summary: When to Call Emergency Services Immediately

Call your hospital's NICU emergency line, the pediatrician's after-hours line, or emergency services (ambulance) if:
1. The baby has stopped breathing for more than 20 seconds or turns blue.
2. The baby's rectal temperature is above 38.0 degrees Celsius or below 36.5 degrees Celsius.
3. The baby vomits green (bile-colored) material.
4. The baby has blood in the stool or vomit.
5. The baby is having a seizure.
6. The baby is completely limp, unresponsive, or impossible to wake.
7. The baby has severe chest retractions with every breath.
8. The baby has a bulging fontanelle when calm and upright.
