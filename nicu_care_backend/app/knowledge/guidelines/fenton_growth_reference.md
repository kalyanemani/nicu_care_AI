# Growth Monitoring for Preterm Infants: Fenton Charts and WHO Standards

## Why Growth Monitoring Is Different for Preterm Babies

Full-term infants (born at 37 to 42 weeks gestation) are monitored using the standard WHO Child Growth Standards, which begin at birth (assumed to be approximately 40 weeks post-menstrual age). These charts are NOT appropriate for plotting preterm infants during their initial months because a baby born at 28 weeks is expected to be much smaller than a baby born at 40 weeks. Using standard WHO charts would falsely label almost every preterm baby as severely underweight.

Instead, preterm infant growth is monitored using the FENTON Preterm Growth Charts, which cover gestational ages from 22 weeks through 50 weeks post-menstrual age. At 50 weeks post-menstrual age (approximately 10 weeks corrected age), the Fenton charts align with and transition seamlessly into the WHO Child Growth Standards.

## Fenton Growth Chart Parameters

The Fenton charts track three key anthropometric measurements, each plotted against post-menstrual age (PMA):

### Weight (grams)
- The most frequently measured parameter.
- Measured using a digital infant scale accurate to the nearest 5 grams.
- Expected weight gain for preterm infants: approximately 15 to 30 grams per day once consistent growth is established.
- Weight should be measured at the same time of day, in the same clothing (or nude), on the same scale for consistency.

### Length (centimeters)
- Measured using an infant length board (not a tape measure, which is inaccurate).
- Expected length gain: approximately 0.8 to 1.1 centimeters per week for preterm infants.
- Length is typically measured at clinic visits, not at home, because accurate measurement requires two people and a proper board.

### Head Circumference (centimeters)
- Measured using a non-stretchable tape measure placed around the largest circumference of the head (above the eyebrows and ears, around the occipital prominence at the back).
- Expected head growth: approximately 0.5 to 1.0 centimeters per week for preterm infants.
- Head circumference is an important indicator of brain growth. Abnormally rapid head growth may indicate hydrocephalus; abnormally slow growth may indicate microcephaly or inadequate nutrition.

## Understanding Growth Percentiles

Each measurement is plotted on the growth chart and assigned a percentile, which indicates how the baby compares to other babies of the same post-menstrual age and sex.

### Percentile Interpretation
- 50th percentile: Average. Half of babies this age are larger, and half are smaller.
- 10th to 90th percentile: Normal range. Most healthy infants fall within this range.
- 3rd to 10th percentile: Below average but may be normal for a small baby. Requires monitoring for consistent growth velocity (trend).
- Below 3rd percentile: Significantly below average. The pediatrician will evaluate for failure to thrive, inadequate caloric intake, or underlying medical conditions.
- Above 90th percentile: Above average. Usually not concerning unless there is a sudden upward crossing of percentile lines (may indicate fluid retention or overfeeding).

### Growth Velocity Matters More Than a Single Point
- A single measurement tells you where the baby is at one moment. The TREND over time (growth velocity) tells you whether the baby is growing adequately.
- A baby who is consistently at the 15th percentile and growing along that curve is healthy. A baby who drops from the 50th percentile to the 10th percentile over 4 weeks needs urgent nutritional evaluation.
- Catch-up growth (crossing upward across percentile lines) is expected and desired for most preterm infants during the first 1 to 2 years.

## Expected Growth Milestones by Corrected Age

### Term Equivalent (40 weeks PMA / 0 weeks corrected age)
- Weight: approximately 2500 to 3500 grams (5.5 to 7.7 pounds).
- Length: approximately 47 to 52 centimeters.
- Head circumference: approximately 33 to 36 centimeters.

### 3 Months Corrected Age
- Weight: approximately 4500 to 6500 grams (10 to 14 pounds).
- Expected to have doubled or nearly doubled birth weight (if born very preterm).
- Social smile, tracks objects with eyes, holds head briefly during tummy time.

### 6 Months Corrected Age
- Weight: approximately 6000 to 8500 grams (13 to 19 pounds).
- Rolls from back to front, reaches for objects, sits with support, laughs.
- Readiness signs for starting solid foods (good head control, interest in food).

### 12 Months Corrected Age
- Weight: approximately 8000 to 11000 grams (18 to 24 pounds).
- Expected to have roughly tripled birth weight.
- Pulls to stand, cruises along furniture, says a few words, picks up small objects with pincer grasp.

## When to Worry About Growth

Contact the pediatrician if:
- The baby has not regained birth weight by 2 weeks after discharge (most babies regain birth weight by 10 to 14 days of age).
- Weight gain is consistently less than 15 grams per day over a 1-week period.
- The baby drops more than 2 major percentile lines on the growth chart (for example, from the 50th to below the 10th percentile).
- Head circumference is growing too fast (more than 1.5 centimeters per week) or too slowly (less than 0.5 centimeters per week over several weeks).
- The baby's length or head circumference percentile is very different from their weight percentile (disproportionate growth may indicate a nutritional or medical issue).

## Transitioning from Fenton to WHO Charts

At approximately 50 weeks post-menstrual age (10 weeks corrected age), the Fenton charts end and growth should be plotted on the standard WHO Child Growth Standards using the CORRECTED age. The WHO charts are used from this point forward through 2 years of age for corrected age assessments, and from 2 to 5 years using chronological age.

## Home Weight Monitoring Tips

- If the pediatrician recommends home weighing, use a dedicated digital infant scale (accurate to 5 grams or better).
- Weigh the baby at the same time each day (ideally before the morning feed), in the same diaper or nude.
- Record the weight, date, and time in a log. Share this log at every pediatric and neonatal follow-up visit.
- Do NOT weigh the baby after every single feed. Daily or twice-weekly weigh-ins are sufficient and reduce parental anxiety. Feed-to-feed weight fluctuations are normal and not meaningful.
